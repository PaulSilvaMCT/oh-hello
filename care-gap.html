The reality behind the authorization

The
Care Gap.

Andrea currently receives 35 authorized hours of home care each week.

That number sounds meaningful until you understand what Andrea needs during all of the other hours of her life.

35
authorized home-care hours per week

Hours on a schedule are not the same thing as adequate care.

What gets missed

Andrea does not stop needing help when the aide leaves.

Much of Andrea's support is not a single task that can be checked off a list. It is the ongoing presence of another person who notices, prompts, redirects and responds.

Meals and nutrition

Andrea cannot reliably manage meal preparation and food choices without help. A prepared meal does not guarantee that she will eat it or make an appropriate substitute.

Household safety

Andrea may not recognize when something in the home is wrong, may leave water running, and may fail to report problems that require immediate attention.

Personal care

Hygiene, clothing, continence needs and daily routines can require prompting, monitoring and redirection.

Judgment and supervision

The absence of a scheduled physical task does not mean Andrea is suddenly able to recognize risk, make safe decisions or respond appropriately when something happens.

The difference that matters

Task completion is not the same as supervision.

A care plan may describe bathing, cooking, cleaning, dressing or other identifiable tasks.

Andrea's needs extend beyond those individual tasks. She needs someone capable of observing what is happening around her, recognizing when something is wrong and helping her respond appropriately.

Presence is not the same as care.

A caregiver can be physically inside the house and still fail to provide meaningful supervision, engagement, redirection or support.

For Andrea, the quality and continuity of the relationship matter too. Familiar caregivers learn her routines, understand her behavior and are more likely to recognize when something is changing.

What the uncovered hours can mean

Everyday situations can become safety problems.

These are examples of the kinds of issues Andrea's family has observed when adequate supervision is not available.

Food can be discarded.

A meal can be prepared for Andrea and later end up uneaten or thrown away.

Problems may go unreported.

Andrea may not recognize that a household fixture, appliance or other part of her environment needs attention.

Routine disruption matters.

Sudden changes in caregivers or familiar routines can lead to behaviors that require additional guidance and redirection.

Family is not a service plan

The gaps have been absorbed by family.

For years, the difference between the care Andrea receives and the care Andrea actually needs has largely been absorbed by family.

Her brother became the person filling many of those gaps. After suffering a stroke, his ability to monitor, supervise and physically manage Andrea's needs became significantly limited.

His wife, Marina, works in Philadelphia and travels to New York on weekends to be with the family and help. Her support is meaningful, but it cannot replace a sustainable professional care structure.

Love can support a care plan.

Respite is care, too

Caregivers cannot remain on duty forever.

A sustainable care system must protect Andrea while also allowing the family members supporting her to rest, recover, work, maintain relationships and live ordinary human lives.

Andrea's brother and Marina have not had a weekend away together in approximately four to five years.

Respite is not a luxury. It is part of what makes long-term caregiving sustainable.

The central problem

The system sees authorized hours. Andrea lives the hours in between.

When a decision is made about home care, the question should not simply be whether a form has been completed or whether a list of individual tasks fits inside an authorization.

The question should be whether the resulting plan allows this particular person to live safely and with dignity.

Andrea's family believes her current level of authorized care does not adequately reflect her actual functional and supervision needs.

What we are asking for

A care plan built around Andrea.

  • Enough professional home-care support to match Andrea's real functional needs.
  • Recognition that supervision, prompting and redirection are meaningful care needs.
  • Greater continuity and quality in the caregivers assigned to her.
  • A sustainable respite structure for the family members who have been filling the uncovered hours.
  • A process that evaluates Andrea as an individual, not merely as a collection of checkboxes.
