The public record

Accountability.

This page exists for one reason: to create a clear public record of what Andrea needed, what was requested, what the system required, what decisions were made and what happened afterward.

Our rule

We will not speculate about motives. We will document actions, requirements, decisions, responses, delays and consequences.

Andrea is not a case that needs to fit a process. The process is supposed to serve Andrea.

How this record works

Facts first.

Each entry in this record is intended to answer the same basic questions.

What was needed?

What care, service, assessment, accommodation or assistance was Andrea actually seeking?

What was required?

What forms, evaluations, records, deadlines or procedures were imposed by the agency, plan or provider?

What happened?

What was submitted, delayed, denied, approved, ignored, reassigned or returned?

What was the consequence?

What did the decision mean in Andrea's actual life and for the family members trying to keep her safe?

Selected history

How we got here.

This timeline will grow as documents, correspondence and dates are assembled. The entries below are based on the family's records and firsthand experience.

October 2020

Andrea's father dies.

Andrea had lived with her parents throughout their lives. Her father died from cancer in October 2020.

93 days later

Andrea's mother dies.

Her mother died only 93 days after her husband. There was no established long-term transition plan for Andrea's care.

After 2020

Andrea returns to New York.

Andrea was brought back to the childhood family home in New York, where her brother began trying to reconstruct her medical, functional and service history.

Service application process

A required psychosocial evaluation becomes too old while the process continues.

The family paid out of pocket for a psychosocial evaluation because it was required as part of an application for services.

By the time the evaluation reached the appropriate point in the process, approximately 13 months had passed. The family was then told that the evaluation could not be accepted because it was more than 12 months old.

Approximately two years

A stable caregiver relationship is established.

Through the CDPAP program, the family was able to select a caregiver who developed a successful long-term relationship with Andrea.

The arrangement provided meaningful continuity and allowed the authorized hours to be concentrated into longer weekday shifts.

Recent change

The long-term caregiver can no longer continue.

After approximately two years, the caregiver was no longer able to continue working because her employment authorization was no longer valid.

The loss of a familiar caregiver disrupted a system that had been working comparatively well for Andrea.

After caregiver loss

The same authorized hours are redistributed.

Without a replacement available through the family's existing CDPAP arrangement, Andrea's 35 authorized weekly hours had to be distributed differently.

The family was not permitted to concentrate those hours into the weekday schedule they believed worked best for Andrea and for the household.

Recent weekend shift

A family member observes inadequate engagement during an aide shift.

Andrea's brother reports that he came downstairs without being noticed and observed a weekend aide scrolling on her phone for approximately 15 minutes while seated near Andrea as Andrea ate.

He reports that during that period there was no meaningful interaction with Andrea.

Ongoing

Advocates encounter the same barriers.

Several advocates have attempted to help the family obtain appropriate services. According to the family, those efforts have so far encountered many of the same bureaucratic obstacles without producing a sustainable solution.

September 2026

The family begins public outreach.

After years of unsuccessful attempts to secure adequate assistance, the family began contacting disability advocates, aging organizations, elected officials, television newsrooms, print media and other potential sources of help.

Oh, Hello grew from that effort.

When process becomes the problem

A requirement can be technically satisfied and still fail the person it was supposed to help.

The psychosocial evaluation

The family was required to obtain a psychosocial evaluation as part of an application for services.

The evaluation was obtained at the family's expense. Approximately 13 months later, the family was told that the document could no longer be accepted because evaluations had to be less than 12 months old.

This is the kind of experience Oh, Hello intends to document: not simply whether a rule exists, but whether the process surrounding that rule produces a reasonable outcome for the person seeking help.

Right of response

Accountability includes the opportunity to answer.

Oh, Hello's accountability policy

  • We distinguish between documented facts, firsthand observations and opinion.
  • We do not knowingly publish private identifying information that is unnecessary to understanding Andrea's situation.
  • Personal medical identifiers, insurance numbers, home addresses and similar information will be redacted.
  • Individual caregivers will generally not be identified publicly. Accountability is directed primarily toward the organizations responsible for training, supervision, staffing and service delivery.
  • Organizations and individuals named in this record are invited to provide corrections, context or responses.
  • Material factual corrections will be made when supported by evidence.
  • Substantive responses may be published alongside the relevant entry so readers can evaluate the record fairly.

We are not asking anyone to take our word for it. We are building the record.
